Road of

Three women’s journeys in defying the Nazis

Behind walls like this one, a resistance was born.

The theme of return

Three women had a way out. Frumka was asked to stay behind. Vladka held papers that could hide her. Hannah had already escaped. Each one turned back.

  • Frumka Płotnicka1914–1943
  • Vladka Meed1921–2012
  • Hannah Senesh1921–1944

This exhibition follows three Jewish women who risked everything to resist the Nazis. At each of their turning points, you will be asked to stand in her place.

1933Adolf Hitler becomes chancellor of Germany.

1939Germany invades Poland. The persecution of Jews begins almost at once.

01

First the walls.
Then the underground.

Sealed in

Jews were forced into ghettos—parts of cities walled off and isolated, designed to weaken the people trapped inside. The Warsaw ghetto was far larger than many imagine, and witnesses describe it as desperately overcrowded.10 Picture your own neighborhood with a wall around it, and everyone you know inside.

Organized from within

Inside these walls, resistance took shape. The Jewish Fighting Organization (ŻOB), founded in the Warsaw ghetto, sent fighters to other ghettos to spark new movements. Resistance meant smuggling in weapons, publishing calls to fight, and urging people to ignore Nazi orders to report to the Umschlagplatz for “resettlement.” Most of it was led by youth groups—run by teenagers.

30,000

Jews escaped the ghettos and deportations to start or join resistance groups.5

Why women?

Women could go where men could not.

Many Jewish men were physically conspicuous as Jews, and after years in religious schools they spoke little Polish. Young women had often attended public schools. They spoke Polish fluently and were not easily recognized as Jewish.37 They became couriers—the underground’s lifeline between ghettos cut off from the world.

02 · Frumka Płotnicka · 1914–1943

Summer 1939 · Stand in her place

Your papers to emigrate to the Land of Israel are ready. Then the leaders of your movement ask you to stay behind in Warsaw.

Many would have left.

You chose as she did.

Frumka stayed.

A destiny decided

Frumka was born in 1914 into a Hasidic family in Belarus, one of five children. As a teenager she joined the Zionist movement He-Haluz, which trained young Jews for life on a kibbutz. When its leaders asked her to remain in Europe as emissary to the Warsaw branch, she agreed. It was a decision that would decide her destiny.11

A living archive

When the movement’s leadership fled east, Frumka stayed in Warsaw. Her “Aryan” features let her pass as a Pole, and she became a courier, carrying news of deportations and extermination camps to ghettos that had no other way to know.12 She was a living archive—like the Oneg Shabbat, the secret record of ghetto life that Emanuel Ringelblum hid in milk cans for future generations.14

Buried for the future

As the final deportations drew near, the archive was buried in the ghetto, to be uncovered by those who came after. Today it is one of the most important sources on ghetto life—a testimony to what happened to European Jewry. Frumka carried the same kind of knowledge, and the same purpose: that the world should know.15

She would not leave

After the mass deportations from Warsaw, the underground sent Frumka to Będzin to lead the uprising there. She had several chances to be rescued. She refused to leave her comrades—though she made sure the others had passports if they needed them. Frumka Płotnicka was killed in the liquidation of the Będzin ghetto on August 1, 1943.13

Her last testimony

Frumka’s missions are recorded in a final letter the Będzin fighters wrote to colleagues already in the Land of Israel. Its authors were murdered, but copies survived. Reading it, you feel their grief as they list their murdered comrades—and their calm, having faced death so many times, about their own fate.18

What would you have done?
Would you have gone back?

The last letter from Będzin 1943 · Illustration · Original at the Jewish Historical Institute, Warsaw

03 · Vladka Meed · 1921–2012

Warsaw · Stand in her place

Your father has died in the ghetto. Your mother, sister, and brother have been deported to Treblinka. You are alone.20 You hold papers that say you are a Polish girl named Stanisława Wachalska.

Many would have hidden.

You chose as she did.

Vladka went back.
Again and again.

Stanisława
Wachalska

Born Feigele Peltel, she was chosen by the resistance to live on the “Aryan side” under this false name.23 From there she obtained bullets, dynamite, and guns, and smuggled them into the ghetto. With these papers she could have simply hidden. Instead she crossed back and forth, again and again, to help the Jews still inside.

Hiding in plain sight

Picture her: a young woman in a summer dress, holding a handbag. Nothing about her would make a passerby look twice. That was the point. Vladka grew up in a traditional Jewish family in Warsaw and was active in socialist youth movements as a teenager. Now her ordinary look was her disguise.20

Carrying children out

Part of her work was smuggling children out of the ghetto and finding them safe houses. It was never easy: the right people had to be found, at the right price—people who would not betray a Jewish child to the Nazis.19 This shoe, unearthed in the ghetto’s ruins,21 stands for every child Vladka carried to safety.22

Ordered to bear witness

The head of the underground ordered her not to join the uprising. She was to survive and testify to her comrades’ heroism.24 From the Polish side she watched the ghetto burn.32 Vladka reached New York in 1946 and devoted her life to Holocaust education, leading seminars that trained American teachers on the Holocaust and Jewish resistance. She died in 2012.

04 · Hannah Senesh · 1921–1944

1943 · Stand in her place

You left Hungary four years ago. You are safe on a kibbutz by the sea. News reaches you of the tragedy in Europe, and the British army is looking for volunteers to parachute behind enemy lines.

Many would have stayed.

You chose as she did.

Hannah came back.

1939She leaves Hungary, alone.

1944She comes back.

This suitcase carried her out of Hungary in 1939.26 She had escaped. She chose to return to help her nation and her family—and paid for that choice with her life.

The suitcase 1939 · Illustration · Original at the National Library of Israel, Senesh Family Archive

Codename “Hagar”

Hannah was born in Hungary in 1921. Feeling the weight of antisemitic restrictions, she emigrated alone in 1939 to the Land of Israel, where she helped establish Kibbutz Sdot Yam. In 1943, hearing of the tragedy in Europe, she volunteered as a paratrooper in the British army. Her codename was “Hagar.”25

Behind enemy lines

Her mission: cross enemy lines, make contact with partisans, gather intelligence, and aid Allied pilots forced to bail out over enemy territory. In 1944 she was dropped into Croatia, where she worked with the partisans for several months.25

Twenty-three

In June 1944 she was caught crossing into her native Hungary. Imprisoned and tortured, she revealed nothing. Tried as a British spy and a traitor to Hungary, she was executed in November 1944, at the age of 23.25 In her pocket was a note to her mother. It thanks her, and says that “words are not necessary.”27

“Words are not necessary.”

Hannah Senesh, in her last note to her mother, 194428

05

What counts as resistance?

Did it make
a difference?

Scholars disagree about what “resistance” means—and so about whether Jews went “like sheep to the slaughter.” Read both views. Where do you stand?

Raul Hilberg

Resistance is armed force.

The foremost Holocaust historian of his day argued that Jewish life did not lend itself to physical resistance. Without disparaging the fighters, he saw them as outliers: mostly young adults who had never conformed to traditional Jewish life.

Yehuda Bauer

Resistance is keeping “body and soul together.”35

By this measure, resistance included smuggling, Jews helping one another, and upholding religious law and custom as fully as they could.36

The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising

Armed resistance did hinder the Nazis. In 1943, when the Germans began a new phase of deportations, the underground fought back and brought liquidation to a temporary halt. The uprising became an example for other ghettos. In the end the fighters were outnumbered and overpowered, and many were killed.29

Other resistance happened every day: aiding neighbors, and holding on to Jewish principles.30 If that counts as resistance, then on that front Jews triumphed over the Nazis.

This exhibition focuses on armed, physical resistance—not to diminish other forms, but because its scope had to end somewhere.

“The story of the Jewish woman will be a glorious page in the history of Jewry during the present war.”

Dr. Emanuel Ringelblum, Notes from the Warsaw Ghetto17

Three women had a chance at survival. All three returned to fight. Only one lived to tell her story. It is our responsibility to make sure future generations know their courage and their sacrifice.

Now, your turn

  1. If you witnessed injustice, would you speak up?
  2. Would you act if it meant risking harm to yourself?
  3. Given the chance to save yourself, would you return to the danger to help others?
  4. When faced with oppression, what do you do?
